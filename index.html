Weigh what you write proposal Motivation Questions Design

Research proposal · October 2026

Do reasoning models weigh what they write?

If a model never writes down what it knows, does that knowledge still count?

A model may know that Lyme disease is common in Connecticut in July, yet a case that says only “July, Connecticut” never states that prior. We ask whether such unstated knowledge still moves the answer as the model thinks longer, and whether it first has to be written into the trace. We measure its weight against the Bayesian value of 1, look for where the behavior comes from in RL training, and train the weight toward 1.

  1. RQ1How much does it count?The weight on the model’s own unstated prior, against Bayes’ 1, across thinking budgets.
  2. RQ2Only once written?Fix whether the trace states the prior, at 1 or 0, and see how much effect is left.
  3. RQ3More if written more?Repeat a sentence k times, early or late in the trace.
  4. RQ4Where does it come from?The same measures across OLMo-3’s SFT, DPO and RLVR checkpoints.
  5. RQ5Can RL fix it?Reward the normative posterior at several reasoning lengths; test on held-out disease pairs.

One case, two contexts

Each item is a choice between two diagnoses. The symptoms stay fixed. Only a context cue changes: a place, a season, an age, never a rate or a prevalence. The cue moves the model’s own prior, and Bayes’ rule says how far the answer should move.

Four answers, four fingerprints

We fix four hypotheses before the first run. They predict different patterns on the same three curves, and they can hold in part at once, so we report graded results rather than a winner.

Motivation

Reasoning erases unstated knowledge in some studies and strengthens it in others. Nobody has separated the two routes on a task with a right answer.

known
  • Longer reasoning can erase what the prompt leaves unstated. An unstated but sensible prior loses weight as the budget grows (Gema et al., TMLR 2025; Qwen3-32B goes the other way); thinking cancels default pragmatic readings (Kou et al. 2026); long reasoning before search uses retrieved evidence worse (KID, 2026).
  • Reasoning can also strengthen parametric knowledge. Recall gains come mostly from facts written into the trace (Thinking to Recall, COLM 2026), and reasoning models lean less on context and more on memory (Lost-in-the-Later, 2025).
  • Information can act without being written: attributes in the prompt change diagnoses while the chain of thought never mentions them (Bürger et al. 2026).
missing
  • For knowledge that lives only in the weights, no one has separated the written route from the silent one.
  • No one has scored that knowledge’s weight against the Bayesian value as the thinking budget grows.
  • “Longer reasoning ignores base rates” has no direct evidence. Gema et al. use a non-medical regression, DiagRare’s budget runs are confounded by truncation, and AutoDiscovery varies the amount of evidence, not the length of reasoning.

One hypothesis could explain both directions: reasoning models weigh what they write. If diagnostic traces spend most of their length restating symptoms, an unwritten prior would be crowded out; recall traces write the facts out, so memory gains. In Gema et al.’s appendix example the prior is written once while a spurious feature is discussed again and again, and the prior loses.

Diagnosis is a clean test bed. Bayes gives the right weight. Pre-test probability really does change with setting and region, so a cue can move the prior inside one case. Traces list the symptoms one by one; how often they also state prevalence is one of the first things we measure. It is also a live problem: without a prevalence cue, GPT-4o over-diagnoses rare emergencies (Safranek et al. 2026), and whether LLMs name the right diagnosis tracks how much literature a disease has, not how common it is (Ríos-Hoyo 2024). Whether longer reasoning makes this better or worse has not been measured.

Our earlier work built a pipeline from Bayes nets to text to continual pretraining, which none of the closest competitors above has. It can implant a known prior into a model, which would give the repair an exact ground truth (stretch experiment S1).

Goal and questions

The goal is a rule for how reasoning models use what they know, and a method that fixes the misuse. Diagnosis, DDXPlus, prefill and suppression are tools, not the goal.

Experiment design

In each trial the symptoms are fixed, a context cue moves the prior, we intervene on whether the trace states that prior, and we read the log-odds of the answer. The diagnosis part needs inference only; the origin part uses public checkpoints; the repair is RL.

terms used below
  • Own prior x̂. The model’s prior for A over B in one context, asked in a separate chat: as a stated share (x̂decl, two wordings, 10 draws each), and as its answer to the same case with the symptoms removed (x̂beh).
  • Evidence ℓ. The log-likelihood ratio of the evidence in the case. It has to be known for the slope to have a Bayesian reference; items use four levels, near ±0.7 and ±2.5.
  • βimpl, βevid, ρ. Slopes of the answer’s log-odds on x̂ and on ℓ within a disease pair; Bayes gives 1 for each. ρ = βimpl / βevid is the main measure.
  • Mention M. Whether the trace states the prior, judged by a regex plus an LLM judge and checked against human labels. “Silent” means unstated: the cue’s effect may still travel through other content in the trace (a question about tick bites, say), which the definition of a mention has to take into account.
  • Budget b. The length of one reasoning trace. We study sequential thinking only and give the model no tools: search would look the prevalence up, and Python would force the prior into a number.

The estimate

ypcs(b) = βimpl(b) · x̂pc + βevid(b) · ℓs + αp + εpcsρ(b) = βimpl(b) / βevid(b)

Here y is the log-odds of A in the answer, x̂ the model’s own prior log-odds, p the disease pair, c the context, s the evidence level, and αp a pair fixed effect. The evidence is the same across contexts, so estimating βimpl needs no likelihoods at all. Noise in x̂ is corrected with ORIV (Gillen, Snowberg & Yariv 2019): the draws are split into halves and each half instruments the other. With few disease pairs, intervals for changes in ρ come from a jackknife over pairs, with a wild cluster bootstrap as a check.

A slope of 1 rests on assumptions we keep in view. The cue should move the prior but not how likely the evidence is, which age cues can violate. ℓ is a reference value, not the model’s own likelihood, so we read the change in ρ with budget before its distance from 1. And x̂ is elicited without thinking, so we elicit it again after long thinking to check for drift.

Items and models

The first study runs two open models: Qwen3-8B at six thinking levels (none; 512 and 2,048 tokens, interrupted; natural length; one and two “Wait” continuations) and gpt-oss at three effort levels.

Intervening on the trace

same item, same budget, three arms
prefill the model’s own prior sentence → M = 1 prefill a neutral sentence of equal length (control) suppress on-policy: resample any sentence that states the prior → M = 0
  • The prefilled sentence is the model’s own words, generated in another chat.
  • Suppression resamples one sentence at a time, as in Thought Branches, because hand-edited traces give near-zero, unstable effects. We report how often the suppressed content comes back.
  • Estimands: the controlled direct effects CDE1(b) and CDE0(b), the prior’s slope with the mention held at 1 or 0, and the share that flows through writing, in the chain-of-thought mediation framework of Zaman & Srivastava (ACL 2026).
  • Writing the prior into the prompt is only a secondary control. In Yamin et al. (2026), writing a model’s own stated probability (a posterior) back into its prompt changed its decisions.
dose and position
  • Insert k ∈ {0, 1, 2, 4} restatements that add no information (a symptom, or the prior, in the model’s words), early or late in the trace.
  • If log-odds track k, the rule sharpens to count, not weigh. Position tests early commitment.
task framing
  • One arm adds “use your general knowledge, including how common each disease is”.
  • It separates cannot use the prior from will not, as when a model treats the case as a closed world.
three budget protocols
  • Interrupted: Qwen3 thinks up to N tokens (512 or 2,048), then gets the official wrap-up line and answers. These levels fill in the curve; the change Δρ is defined on the uninterrupted levels.
  • Planned: the model knows its budget (gpt-oss low, medium, high effort).
  • Extended: when Qwen3 tries to stop, the trace forks; one branch answers, the other gets “Wait” and thinks on, as in s1. One trace yields paired answers at three lengths.
  • Every level reports overflow, real token counts and parse rates.
origin (RQ4)
  • OLMo-3-7B-Think publishes SFT and DPO models and 55 RLVR checkpoints (steps 25 to 1375), plus RL-Zero runs from the base model on math, code, IF, general and mixed data.
  • Inference only. The prediction is two-sided: RL on closed-world math may teach “use only what is written”, but RL is also reported to improve recall of parametric facts.

The repair (RQ5)

RL toward the normative posterior
  • Target. Real disease pairs with context cues. Prior from epidemiology, likelihood from DDXPlus, posterior by Bayes. On real pairs this rewards accurate knowledge as well as correct use, and training may move x̂ itself. Stretch: an implanted world of fictional diseases, where the posterior is exact and only use is tested.
  • Reward. A proper scoring rule (Brier or log score) against the target posterior, scored at several cut points of the same trace and at its end, so the weight holds at every length.
  • Arms. Final-answer reward only (does RL learn to write the prior, or to use it silently?); the same plus a process reward for stating the prior; plain right-or-wrong RLVR.
  • Guard. Track βevid. Buying prior weight by ignoring the symptoms is a reward hack, and the posterior target penalizes it.
  • Setup. GRPO with LoRA on Qwen3-4B, then 8B, with math RLVR data mixed in.
  • Test. Held-out pairs, real cases and one non-medical base-rate domain, with no loss on math and general benchmarks.

Closest methods: Sun et al. (2026) reward the true posterior, but base rates come from examples in the prompt; VAKE (2026) trains a model to write knowledge out and then to use it unwritten, for factual QA; RLCR (2025) calibrates confidence, not the use of a prior. Two searches found nothing that combines a prior held only in the weights, a posterior reward, robustness to reasoning length and a check on whether the prior is written.
