Weigh what you write proposal Motivation Questions Design

Research proposal · October 2026

Do reasoning models weigh what they write?

If a model never writes down what it knows, does that knowledge still count?

A model may know that a heart attack is rare at 31 and common at 68, yet an exam case that gives only the age never states that prior. We ask whether reasoning models use such knowledge the way Bayes’ rule says they should, and whether thinking longer makes them more Bayesian or only makes them talk about it. In a world of fictional diseases that we train into a model, the truth is known, so we can tell errors of knowledge from errors of use, and then train the use.

  1. RQ1Do they use what they know?Change only the age in a real exam case. The answer should move as far as the model’s own prior moves, with or without the telltale findings.
  2. RQ2Does thinking make them Bayesian?The same checks at several thinking levels, with and without a reminder to weigh how common each disease is.
  3. RQ3What happens in the trace?Does the trace state the prior, use the prior it states, or count the findings twice? Write the prior in or suppress it, and watch the answer.
  4. RQ4Knowledge or use?In a world of fictional diseases implanted by training, split the error into what the model learned and how it uses it.
  5. RQ5Can training fix the use?RL toward the exact posterior in the implanted world and toward coherence on real cases; test on exam cases.

One case, two ages

Each item is a real exam case reduced to a choice between two diagnoses. Only a context cue changes, here the patient’s age; the findings stay the same. The age moves the model’s own prior, and Bayes’ rule says how far the answer should move.

Four answers, four fingerprints

We fix four hypotheses before the first run. They predict different patterns on the same three lines, and they can hold in part at once, so we report graded results rather than a winner.

Motivation

Reasoning erases unstated knowledge in some studies and strengthens it in others. Nobody has separated the two routes on a task with a right answer.

known
  • Longer reasoning can erase what the prompt leaves unstated. An unstated but sensible prior loses weight as the budget grows (Gema et al., TMLR 2025; Qwen3-32B goes the other way); thinking cancels default pragmatic readings (Kou et al. 2026); long reasoning before search uses retrieved evidence worse (KID, 2026).
  • Reasoning can also strengthen parametric knowledge. Recall gains come mostly from facts written into the trace (Thinking to Recall, COLM 2026), and reasoning models lean less on context and more on memory (Lost-in-the-Later, 2025).
  • Information can act without being written: attributes in the prompt change diagnoses while the chain of thought never mentions them (Bürger et al. 2026).
missing
  • For knowledge that lives only in the weights, no one has separated the written route from the silent one.
  • No one has scored that knowledge’s weight against the Bayesian value as the thinking budget grows.
  • No one has checked whether a model’s answers agree with its own stated base rates when only the context of a case changes, or told errors of knowledge from errors of use against a known truth.
  • “Longer reasoning ignores base rates” has no direct evidence. Gema et al. use a non-medical regression, DiagRare’s budget runs are confounded by truncation, and AutoDiscovery varies the amount of evidence, not the length of reasoning.

One hypothesis could explain both directions: reasoning models weigh what they write. If diagnostic traces spend most of their length restating symptoms, an unwritten prior would be crowded out; recall traces write the facts out, so memory gains. In Gema et al.’s appendix example the prior is written once while a spurious feature is discussed again and again, and the prior loses. The reverse is also possible: a model may write the prior down, even compute with it, and still not weigh it. Telling these apart needs a check against the model’s own knowledge.

Diagnosis is a clean test bed. Bayes gives the right weight. Pre-test probability really does change with setting and region, so a cue can move the prior inside one case. Clinician-written exam cases supply realistic findings, and we edit only the cue. Traces list the symptoms one by one; how often they also state prevalence is one of the first things we measure. It is also a live problem: without a prevalence cue, GPT-4o over-diagnoses rare emergencies (Safranek et al. 2026), and whether LLMs name the right diagnosis tracks how much literature a disease has, not how common it is (Ríos-Hoyo 2024). Whether longer reasoning makes this better or worse has not been measured.

Our earlier work built a pipeline from Bayes nets to text to continual pretraining, which none of the closest competitors above has. It implants known priors into a model. Here it becomes the second tier of the study, where the truth is known and errors of knowledge and of use can be told apart.

Goal and questions

The goal is a rule for how reasoning models use what they know, and a method that fixes the misuse. Exam cases, the implanted world, prefill and suppression are tools, not the goal.

Experiment design

Two tiers. In real exam cases we change only a context cue and check the answer against the model’s own prior. In an implanted world we also know the truth, so errors of knowledge and of use come apart, and the same world serves as a training environment. The first tier needs inference only.

terms used below
  • Own prior x̂. The model’s odds of X over Y at one age before any findings: its answer, in a separate chat, to a stripped case that keeps only the age, sex, setting, chief complaint and any risk factor that defines the population. Stated priors check it, asked with no case as a share (“among people of this age who have X or Y, what fraction have X?”) and as yearly cases per 100,000, 10 draws each. A stated prior need not be the one a model uses, so the two are compared, not merged.
  • Evidence strength. Full: the exam case as written. Weakened: the findings that best separate X from Y are removed, and nothing is added.
  • Δy, Δx̂, slope, invariance. Δy is the change in the answer’s log-odds of X over Y between two ages, and Δx̂ the change in the own prior. Bayes gives Δy = Δx̂ (a slope of 1), and the same Δy at both evidence strengths.
  • Mention M. Whether the trace states the prior, judged by a regex plus an LLM judge and checked against human labels.
  • Thinking level. The length of one reasoning trace. We study sequential thinking only and give the model no tools: search would look the prevalence up, and Python would force the prior into a number.

The estimate

Δyie(b) = βe(b) · Δx̂i + εieBayes: βfull = βweak = 1

Here i is the item, e the evidence strength and b the thinking level. Only the age changes, so the likelihood of the findings cancels: no item needs a likelihood or a true posterior. Where epidemiology gives the true change Δtrue, the gap splits in two:

Δy − Δtrue = (Δx̂ − Δtrue) + (Δy − Δx̂)knowledge + use

Three assumptions stay in view. The age should change only the prior, not how likely the findings are, so we exclude cases where age changes what a finding means. The stripped case should carry the prior the full case starts from, so we compare it with the stated priors. And exam items may be memorized, so a placebo version moves the age by only two years, where Bayes predicts almost no change. Intervals come from a bootstrap over items.

Items and models

from an exam item to four versions
  • Source: MedQA items that ask for the most likely diagnosis, written for licensing exams.
  • Keep an item if one wrong option is a real alternative whose base rate, relative to the answer, changes with age in a direction epidemiology supports.
  • The age must change without contradiction: no pregnancy, menopause or school details tied to it, unless they can be dropped from every version. Text only.
  • Versions: two ages × full or weakened evidence; asked plainly or with a reminder; two options, with their order balanced; plus a placebo with the age moved by two years.
  • Items are screened and edited by a model that is not under test, checked one by one, then frozen.
what goes in, what comes out
  • In: the case, then “Assume the diagnosis is exactly one of (A) X or (B) Y. Which is more likely, and what is P(A)?”
  • Out: a choice, P(A) and the full trace.
  • In separate chats: the stripped case at each age for the own prior, and the stated-prior questions as a check.
  • From the trace: whether it states how age changes the base rates, the numbers it writes, and whether it computes with them.

The first runs use Qwen3-8B with thinking off and on, and gpt-oss-120b at low and high effort. More families follow (Qwen3-32B, DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B, OLMo-3-7B-Think), with a few API models for the thinking curve.

The implanted world (RQ4)

  • World. Fictional diseases and settings, with designed base rates that change with age, place or season, and designed symptom probabilities: a small Bayes net.
  • Text. The net becomes the kinds of text a model learns medicine from: surveillance bulletins, case reports, textbook entries. No single document states the whole prior.
  • Training. Continued pretraining or LoRA on a reasoning model, with our earlier Bayes-net-to-text pipeline.
  • Test. Probe what the model learned (its own prior against the designed one), then ask cases whose exact posterior we know, and split the error into knowledge and use.
  • Why fictional. The truth is ours to set, nothing can be remembered from the web, and the same cases give the repair an exact reward.

Inside the trace (RQ3)

same item, same budget, three arms
prefill the model’s own prior sentence → M = 1 prefill a neutral sentence of equal length (control) suppress on-policy: resample any sentence that states the prior → M = 0
  • The prefilled sentence is the model’s own words, generated in another chat.
  • Suppression resamples one sentence at a time, as in Thought Branches, because hand-edited traces give near-zero, unstable effects. We report how often the suppressed content comes back.
  • Estimands: the controlled direct effects CDE1(b) and CDE0(b), the prior’s slope with the mention held at 1 or 0, and the share that flows through writing, in the chain-of-thought mediation framework of Zaman & Srivastava (ACL 2026).
  • Writing the prior into the prompt is only a secondary control. In Yamin et al. (2026), writing a model’s own stated probability (a posterior) back into its prompt changed its decisions.
dose and position
  • Insert k ∈ {0, 1, 2, 4} restatements that add no information (a symptom, or the prior, in the model’s words), early or late in the trace.
  • If log-odds track k, the rule sharpens to count, not weigh. Position tests early commitment.
task framing
  • One arm adds “take into account how common each condition is in a patient like this” (RQ2).
  • It separates cannot use the prior from will not, as when a model treats the case as a closed world.
three budget protocols
  • Interrupted: Qwen3 thinks up to N tokens (512 or 2,048), then gets the official wrap-up line and answers. These levels fill in the curve; the main comparison uses the uninterrupted levels.
  • Planned: the model knows its budget (gpt-oss low, medium, high effort).
  • Extended: when Qwen3 tries to stop, the trace forks; one branch answers, the other gets “Wait” and thinks on, as in s1. One trace yields paired answers at three lengths.
  • Every level reports overflow, real token counts and parse rates.
origin (optional)
  • OLMo-3-7B-Think publishes SFT and DPO models and 55 RLVR checkpoints (steps 25 to 1375), plus RL-Zero runs from the base model on math, code, IF, general and mixed data.
  • Inference only. The prediction is two-sided: RL on closed-world math may teach “use only what is written”, but RL is also reported to improve recall of parametric facts.

The repair (RQ5)

RL toward the right use
  • Targets. In the implanted world, the exact posterior. In real cases, where no truth exists, coherence: across two ages, the answer must move as far as the model’s own prior does.
  • Reward. A proper scoring rule (Brier or log score) against the target, scored at several cut points of the same trace and at its end, so the weight holds at every length.
  • Arms. Final-answer reward only (does RL learn to write the prior, or to use it silently?); the same plus a process reward for stating the prior; plain right-or-wrong RLVR.
  • Guard. Track how the answer responds to the findings. Buying prior weight by ignoring the evidence is a reward hack, and both targets penalize it.
  • Setup. GRPO with LoRA on Qwen3-4B, then 8B, with math RLVR data mixed in.
  • Test. Held-out worlds, MedQA cases and one non-medical base-rate domain, with no loss on math and general benchmarks.

Closest methods: Sun et al. (2026) reward the true posterior, but base rates come from examples in the prompt; VAKE (2026) trains a model to write knowledge out and then to use it unwritten, for factual QA; RLCR (2025) calibrates confidence, not the use of a prior. Two searches found nothing that combines a prior held only in the weights, a posterior reward, robustness to reasoning length and a check on whether the prior is written.
